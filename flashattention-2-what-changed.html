FlashAttention-2: What Changed Since FlashAttention

What FlashAttention-2 changes relative to FlashAttention, and what it keeps.

Paper comparison · FlashAttention (Dao et al., 2022) and FlashAttention-2 (Dao, 2023)

This document covers only the differences between two papers:

  • FA1: Dao, Fu, Ermon, Rudra, Ré. FlashAttention: Fast and Memory-Efficient Exact Attention with IO-Awareness. arXiv:2205.14135v2
  • FA2: Dao. FlashAttention-2: Faster Attention with Better Parallelism and Work Partitioning. arXiv:2307.08691v1

Summary

FA2 computes the same exact attention as FA1 at the same asymptotic cost: \(O(N^2 d)\) FLOPs, \(O(N)\) extra memory, and no \(N \times N\) matrix in high-bandwidth memory (HBM). What changes is how the work runs once the data is on the chip.

FA1 removed the HBM traffic of the attention matrix. With that gone, FA1 still reached only 30–50% of the A100's theoretical peak FLOPs/s in the forward pass and 25–35% in the backward pass. Optimized matrix multiply (GEMM) reaches 80–90%. FA2 traces the gap to three causes and makes one change for each:

Cause of the gap in FA1Change in FA2Section of the FA2 paper
Time goes to FLOPs that are not matrix multipliesRestructure the online-softmax update so each step rescales less, and save one statistic per row instead of two§3.1
Streaming multiprocessors (SMs) sit idle when batch × heads is smallParallelize over sequence length as well: one thread block per block of rows (forward) or of columns (backward)§3.2
Warps inside a thread block exchange partial results through shared memorySplit \(\mathbf{Q}\) across warps instead of \(\mathbf{K}\) and \(\mathbf{V}\)§3.3

Together these make FA2 about 2× faster than FA1 (1.7–3.0× across the benchmark settings). FA2 reaches up to 73% of theoretical peak in the forward pass and 63% in the backward pass on an A100.

FA2 also adds block skipping under a causal mask, support for multi-query and grouped-query attention, and hand-tuned block sizes.

Notation

\(N\) is the sequence length and \(d\) the head dimension. \(\mathbf{Q}\) is cut into \(T_r\) blocks \(\mathbf{Q}_i\) of \(B_r\) rows. \(\mathbf{K}\) and \(\mathbf{V}\) are cut into \(T_c\) blocks \(\mathbf{K}_j, \mathbf{V}_j\) of \(B_c\) rows. A tile is the \(B_r \times B_c\) piece \(\mathbf{S}_{ij} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top\). Online softmax keeps two numbers per row: the running max \(m\) and the running sum of exponentials \(\ell\).

1. The bottleneck moved on-chip

FA1's cost model has two memory levels, HBM and on-chip SRAM, and counts the transfers between them. FA1 proved that no exact attention algorithm does asymptotically fewer HBM accesses for all SRAM sizes. FA2 does not improve that count's order of growth. Its gains come from three costs that the model leaves out:

  • The kind of FLOP. An A100 delivers 312 TFLOPs/s of FP16/BF16 matmul on Tensor Cores, but 19.5 TFLOPs/s of non-matmul FP32. One non-matmul FLOP therefore costs as much time as 16 matmul FLOPs.
  • Occupancy: the fraction of the GPU's compute resources in use.
  • Data movement inside one SM, between registers and shared memory.

The last two need a finer execution model than FA1 used. FA1 described a kernel as many threads. FA2 names the levels between a kernel and a thread:

UnitMade ofRuns onHow its members exchange data
Kernelthread blocksthe whole GPUthrough HBM, for example with atomic adds
Thread blockwarps (FA2 uses 4 or 8)one SM (an A100 has 108)by reading and writing shared memory, which is the SM's SRAM
Warp32 threads—fast shuffle instructions; the threads cooperate on one matmul

Sections 2–3 below cover the first cost, section 4 the second, and section 5 the third.

2. Forward pass: the loops swap

FA1 loops over K/V blocks on the outside and Q blocks on the inside. FA2 loops over Q blocks on the outside and K/V blocks on the inside. FA2 credits this swap, and the parallelism it allows, to Phil Tillet's implementation of FlashAttention in Triton.

FA1 (Algorithm 1)
for j in K/V blocks: load Kj, Vj for i in Q blocks: load Qi, Oi, ℓi, mi // HBM update with tile Sij write Oi, ℓi, mi // HBM
FA2 (Algorithm 1)
for i in Q blocks: load Qi; init mi, ℓi, Õi on-chip for j in K/V blocks: load Kj, Vj update with tile Sij write Oi, Li // HBM, once

The swap has three consequences:

  1. A row block's running state stays on-chip. The statistics \(m_i\), \(\ell_i\) and the output accumulator live in SRAM from the first tile to the last. FA1 wrote them to HBM and read them back once per K/V block.
  2. The outer iterations are independent. Softmax and \(\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}\) both reduce over keys within one row, so no row needs another row. Section 4 uses this for parallelism.
  3. Normalization can wait until the end, because the same worker holds the state for the whole row block. Section 3 uses this.
HBM traffic

FA2 does not restate FA1's IO analysis, so this count is mine, read off the two pseudo-codes. Per head, FA1's forward pass moves about \(2Nd + T_c(3Nd + 4N)\) elements, and FA2's moves about \(2Nd + N + 2\,T_r Nd\). The roles swap: FA2 re-reads \(\mathbf{K}\) and \(\mathbf{V}\) once per Q block, reads \(\mathbf{Q}\) once and writes \(\mathbf{O}\) once. Both counts are (number of blocks) × \(\Theta(Nd)\), so the order of growth is unchanged when \(B_r\) is sized as FA1 sized \(B_c\). For equal block sizes FA2 moves about two-thirds as many elements, and none of the repeated traffic is a write.

3. Forward pass: fewer non-matmul FLOPs per step

Each step folds one tile into the row block's state. The two papers do this differently.

FA1, per tile:

\[ \tilde m = \operatorname{rowmax}(\mathbf{S}_{ij}), \qquad \tilde{\mathbf{P}} = \exp(\mathbf{S}_{ij} - \tilde m), \qquad \tilde\ell = \operatorname{rowsum}(\tilde{\mathbf{P}}) \] \[ m^{\text{new}} = \max(m, \tilde m), \qquad \ell^{\text{new}} = e^{m - m^{\text{new}}}\,\ell + \class{gone}{e^{\tilde m - m^{\text{new}}}}\,\tilde\ell \] \[ \mathbf{O}_i \leftarrow \class{gone}{\operatorname{diag}(\ell^{\text{new}})^{-1}}\Big(\class{gone}{\operatorname{diag}(\ell)}\,e^{m - m^{\text{new}}}\,\mathbf{O}_i + \class{gone}{e^{\tilde m - m^{\text{new}}}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{V}_j\Big) \]

Highlighted factors do not appear in FA2's per-tile update.

FA2, per tile:

\[ m^{\text{new}} = \max\big(m, \operatorname{rowmax}(\mathbf{S}_{ij})\big), \qquad \tilde{\mathbf{P}} = \exp(\mathbf{S}_{ij} - m^{\text{new}}) \] \[ \ell \leftarrow e^{m - m^{\text{new}}}\,\ell + \operatorname{rowsum}(\tilde{\mathbf{P}}), \qquad \tilde{\mathbf{O}}_i \leftarrow \operatorname{diag}\big(e^{m - m^{\text{new}}}\big)\,\tilde{\mathbf{O}}_i + \tilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{V}_j \]

FA2, once per row block, after the last tile:

\[ \mathbf{O}_i = \operatorname{diag}(\ell)^{-1}\,\tilde{\mathbf{O}}_i, \qquad L_i = m + \log \ell \]

There are three differences:

  1. Normalization is deferred. FA1 keeps \(\mathbf{O}_i\) normalized after every step, so each step multiplies the old output by \(\ell\) and divides the result by \(\ell^{\text{new}}\). FA2 accumulates the un-normalized sum \(\tilde{\mathbf{O}}_i\) and divides by \(\ell\) once.
  2. The tile is exponentiated against the running max. FA1 exponentiates against the tile's own max \(\tilde m\), then corrects the new term by \(e^{\tilde m - m^{\text{new}}}\). FA2 merges the max first, so \(\tilde{\mathbf{P}}\) is already on the shared reference and the new term needs no correction. The paper's prose does not mention this change, but it shows in the pseudo-code.
  3. One statistic is saved for the backward pass. FA1 saves \(m\) and \(\ell\). FA2 saves their combination, the log-sum-exp \(L = m + \log\ell\) of the row's scores.

Per row and per step, FA1 computes two correction factors and a reciprocal, then scales two length-\(d\) vectors. FA2 computes one factor and scales one length-\(d\) vector. The exponential, row max and row sum over the tile's entries remain in both.

The un-normalized state stays bounded. Every exponent \(\mathbf{S}_{ij} - m\) is at most 0, so every weight lies in \((0, 1]\) and each row's largest weight is exactly 1. Hence \(1 \le \ell \le N\).

The result is the same as FA1's. After the last tile, \(m\) is the row's global max, \(\tilde{\mathbf{O}}_i = \sum_j \exp(\mathbf{S}_{ij} - m)\,\mathbf{V}_j\) and \(\ell = \operatorname{rowsum}(\exp(\mathbf{S}_i - m))\). Their quotient is \(\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{S}_i)\,\mathbf{V}\).

Reference implementation of the FA2 forward pass

Each slice stands in for a load into SRAM. The listing includes the \(1/\sqrt{d}\) scaling that the paper omits.

import torch

def flash_attention2_forward(Q, K, V, Br=64, Bc=64):
    """FlashAttention-2 forward pass (Algorithm 1) in plain PyTorch. Q, K, V: (N, d)."""
    N, d = Q.shape
    scale = d ** -0.5
    O = torch.zeros_like(Q)                        # output, lives in "HBM"
    L = torch.zeros(N)                             # row-wise logsumexp, saved for backward

    for i in range(0, N, Br):                      # outer loop: Q blocks, independent of each other
        Qi = Q[i:i+Br]                             # "load Q_i into SRAM"
        rows = Qi.shape[0]
        m = torch.full((rows,), -float("inf"))     # running row max        | these three stay
        l = torch.zeros(rows)                      # running row sum        | on-chip for the
        acc = torch.zeros(rows, d)                 # un-normalized output   | whole inner loop

        for j in range(0, N, Bc):                  # inner loop: K/V blocks, sequential per row
            Kj, Vj = K[j:j+Bc], V[j:j+Bc]          # "load K_j, V_j into SRAM"
            S = scale * (Qi @ Kj.T)                # (rows, Bc) tile, never stored
            m_new = torch.maximum(m, S.max(dim=1).values)
            P = torch.exp(S - m_new[:, None])      # exponentiate against the running max
            alpha = torch.exp(m - m_new)           # <= 1: moves the old terms to the new max
            l = alpha * l + P.sum(dim=1)
            acc = alpha[:, None] * acc + P @ Vj    # one row rescale, no division
            m = m_new

        O[i:i+Br] = acc / l[:, None]               # normalize once, write O_i once
        L[i:i+Br] = m + torch.log(l)               # write L_i once
    return O, L

# check against naive attention
torch.manual_seed(0)
Q, K, V = (torch.randn(1000, 64) for _ in range(3))
S = Q @ K.T / 8.0
O, L = flash_attention2_forward(Q, K, V)
print((O - torch.softmax(S, dim=-1) @ V).abs().max())   # O matches softmax(S) V
print((L - torch.logsumexp(S, dim=-1)).abs().max())     # L is the row-wise logsumexp of S
Note

This listing has not been run under PyTorch, which was not available where this document was written. It was run as written against a small pure-Python stand-in for the PyTorch calls it uses, in float64. Both printed errors were below 2e-15. It also matched an independent scalar implementation of naive attention for block sizes that do not divide \(N\). Under PyTorch's default float32, expect errors near float32 rounding instead.

4. Parallelism over sequence length

FA1 uses one thread block per (batch, head) pair, so a kernel launch has batch × heads thread blocks. That uses almost all of an A100's 108 SMs when the product is about 80 or more. Long sequences usually come with small batches, and then most SMs idle. In the paper's benchmark shape at \(N\) = 16k and \(d\) = 128, the batch is 1 and there are 16 heads: 16 thread blocks for 108 SMs.

FA2 adds the sequence dimension to the split:

  • Forward: one thread block per (batch, head, Q block). In the example above with \(B_r = 128\), that is about 2,000 thread blocks. They never communicate.
  • Backward: one thread block per (batch, head, K/V block). They share one output, \(\mathbf{dQ}\).

Under a causal mask, the tiles above the diagonal drop out of both grids (section 7).

The split runs along rows in one pass and along columns in the other, for these reasons:

  • Forward. A row carries running state (\(m\), \(\ell\), \(\tilde{\mathbf{O}}\)) from one K/V block to the next, so one worker must process a row's tiles in order. Different rows share nothing.
  • Backward. There is no running state. Each tile of \(\mathbf{P}\) is recomputed exactly from the saved \(L\), so every tile is independent, and the only question is which gradient sums stay private to a worker. \(\mathbf{dK}_j\) and \(\mathbf{dV}_j\) sum over \(i\), down a column. \(\mathbf{dQ}_i\) sums over \(j\), across a row. A column worker keeps \(\mathbf{dK}_j\) and \(\mathbf{dV}_j\) on-chip and writes each once. It adds its term \(\mathbf{dS}_{ij}\mathbf{K}_j\) into \(\mathbf{dQ}_i\) in HBM with an atomic add, because every other column worker adds to the same \(\mathbf{dQ}_i\). A split by rows would leave two shared outputs instead of one.

The backward loop order is the same as FA1's, with K/V blocks on the outside. FA2 runs those outer iterations concurrently.

5. Work partitioning between warps

Sections 2–4 decide which tiles a thread block computes. Inside the thread block, the 4 or 8 warps must also divide the work on each tile.

  • FA1 splits \(\mathbf{K}_j\) and \(\mathbf{V}_j\) across the warps and lets every warp read \(\mathbf{Q}_i\). The paper calls this the "split-K" scheme. Each warp ends up with a partial sum, and combining the partial sums costs shared-memory writes, a synchronization and shared-memory reads.
  • FA2 splits \(\mathbf{Q}_i\) across the warps and lets every warp read \(\mathbf{K}_j\) and \(\mathbf{V}_j\). Each warp owns complete rows, so it finishes them alone.
Key idea · cut along rows, not keys

This is the rule from section 4 applied one level down. Softmax and \(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}\) both reduce over keys. A cut along keys leaves pieces that must be merged, and a cut along rows leaves pieces that are independent. The FA2 forward pass cuts along rows at both the thread-block level and the warp level.

The name "split-K" comes from GEMM kernels, where K is the inner dimension that the product sums over. This is background from outside the paper. For \(\tilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{V}_j\) that inner dimension is the key index.

Backward pass. FA2 also avoids split-K there. Some synchronization between warps remains, because of the dependencies among \(\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}, \mathbf{O}, \mathbf{dO}, \mathbf{dQ}, \mathbf{dK}, \mathbf{dV}\). The paper gives no more detail.

Block sizes. FA1 derives block sizes from the SRAM size \(M\): \(B_c = \lceil M/4d \rceil\) and \(B_r = \min(\lceil M/4d \rceil, d)\). FA2's pseudo-code takes block sizes as inputs, and the implementation picks them by hand from \(\{64, 128\} \times \{64, 128\}\), depending on the head dimension and the device's shared-memory size. Larger blocks mean fewer shared-memory loads and stores, but need more registers and more shared memory. Past some size, registers spill and the kernel slows down sharply, or the blocks no longer fit and the kernel cannot run. The paper leaves auto-tuning to future work.

6. Backward pass: the changes in one table

The gradient formulas are FA1's: \(\mathbf{dV} = \mathbf{P}^\top\mathbf{dO}\), \(\mathbf{dP} = \mathbf{dO}\,\mathbf{V}^\top\), \(\mathbf{dS} = \mathbf{P} \circ (\mathbf{dP} - D)\), \(\mathbf{dQ} = \mathbf{dS}\,\mathbf{K}\) and \(\mathbf{dK} = \mathbf{dS}^\top\mathbf{Q}\), with \(D = \operatorname{rowsum}(\mathbf{dO} \circ \mathbf{O})\). The paper calls the backward pass "almost the same" as FA1's.

Saved by the forward pass\(\mathbf{O}\), \(m\), \(\ell\)\(\mathbf{O}\), \(L\)
Recomputed tile of \(\mathbf{P}\)\(\operatorname{diag}(\ell_i)^{-1}\exp(\mathbf{S}_{ij} - m_i)\)\(\exp(\mathbf{S}_{ij} - L_i)\)
\(D_i\)computed inside the inner loopcomputed once before the loops and stored as a length-\(N\) vector

Unit of parallelism(batch, head)(batch, head, K/V block)
Update of \(\mathbf{dQ}_i\)read, add, write by the one workeratomic adds from every column worker

The two forms of the recomputed tile are equal, because \(\exp(\mathbf{S} - m - \log\ell) = \exp(\mathbf{S} - m)/\ell\). The FA2 form drops a division per entry and one saved vector per head. The earlier \(D_i\) computation shows only in the pseudo-code.

7. Causal masks and shared K/V heads

Causal mask. A causal mask sets \(S_{ij}\) to \(-\infty\) wherever \(j > i\). FA2 uses the tile grid in two ways:

  1. It skips every tile that lies entirely above the diagonal. For long sequences that is about half the tiles, and attention runs 1.7–1.8× faster than the same attention without the mask.
  2. It applies the mask only to tiles that straddle the diagonal. Tiles entirely below the diagonal need no mask. With square tiles, one tile per row block is masked.

FA1's exact-attention pseudo-code applies the mask function to every tile. FA1 skips tiles only in its block-sparse variant, which is approximate. FA2 applies the same skip to the causal mask, where the result stays exact.

Multi-query and grouped-query attention (MQA, GQA). In these variants several query heads attend to one shared key/value head, which shrinks the KV cache at inference. FA2 indexes into the shared head instead of duplicating \(\mathbf{K}\) and \(\mathbf{V}\). In the backward pass it sums \(\mathbf{dK}\) and \(\mathbf{dV}\) over the query heads that share them.

8. What did not change

  • The output is exact attention, identical to FA1's.
  • The cost is \(O(N^2 d)\) FLOPs and \(O(N)\) extra memory. The extra memory is now one vector \(L\) per head instead of two.
  • The forward pass still tiles the computation and merges tiles with online softmax, inside one fused kernel.
  • The backward pass still recomputes \(\mathbf{S}\) and \(\mathbf{P}\) one tile at a time and uses \(D_i = \mathbf{do}_i^\top \mathbf{o}_i\).
  • Within one row, the forward pass still processes K/V blocks in sequence.

FA2 does not revisit FA1's IO-complexity theorems or its block-sparse attention. FA2's pseudo-code also leaves out the scaling, general masking and dropout that FA1's full algorithms include.

10. Typos in the FA2 paper (v1)

Four places in the paper read differently from what the algorithm does:

  1. Algorithm 1, line 10 prints the factor on the old accumulator as \(\operatorname{diag}(e^{m^{(j-1)} - m^{(j)}})^{-1}\). The inverse should not be there. The factor is at most 1 and scales the old terms down to the new max, as in section 3 above. In a numerical check on random inputs, the form without the inverse matched naive attention to 1e-15, and the printed form was off by more than 1e5. The two-block examples in §2.3.1 and §3.1.1 carry the same stray inverse.
  2. §3.1.1, causal masking, item 2 says no mask is needed for blocks "whose row indices are guaranteed to be strictly less than the column indices". Those are the fully masked blocks. The blocks that need no mask are the ones whose column indices are no greater than their row indices.
  3. Algorithm 2, line 4 gives \(D \in \mathbb{R}^d\). \(D\) has one entry per row, so \(D \in \mathbb{R}^N\).
  4. §4.2 says FA2 yields a 1.3× speedup "compared to FlashAttention-2". It means compared to FlashAttention.
